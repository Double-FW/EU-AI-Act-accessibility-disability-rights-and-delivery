# EU AI Act: accessibility, disability rights and delivery

**A critical review of “Accessibility and the EU AI Act”, its linked resources and the relationship with other legislation**  
**Research cut-off: 11 November 2026 · Language: British English**

## Reading this report

The article’s central recommendation is sound: accessibility belongs within AI governance, procurement, design and operation. However, its simplified account should not be used as an applicability decision or a compliance specification. AI governance needs to address exclusion caused by decisions and data as well as barriers in the interface.

The report distinguishes **legal duties**, **interpretation requiring a service-specific decision**, and **recommended delivery practice**. Recommendations are not presented as additional statutory requirements.

The article was supplied as text without its publication URL or date. That supplied version is the text assessed. All eleven distinct URLs embedded in it were examined, including its two tag indexes. Important supporting legal and standards sources were checked independently. This is not an unlimited recursive crawl of every navigation, promotional or onward link on those websites, nor an assessment of services actually delivered by the organisations concerned.

Double-FW’s current public repository inventory was retrieved. Sixteen relevant repository READMEs and ten specialist files were reviewed. Their guidance is used for delivery methods, not as authority for what EU law requires. Some packages identify themselves as drafts awaiting organisational adoption or independent evaluation; those qualifications remain relevant.

## 1. Overall finding

The AI Act, EAA and AVMSD address connected but different questions:

| Instrument | Central question for this analysis |
| --- | --- |
| AI Act | Is the AI use permissible, appropriately controlled, transparent and supported by the required evidence? |
| EAA | Can disabled consumers access and use the covered product or service, including its information, interactions and support? |
| Web Accessibility Directive (WAD) | Are the covered public-sector websites and apps accessible, with statements, feedback, monitoring and enforcement? |
| Audiovisual Media Services Directive (AVMSD) | Are covered media services becoming continuously and progressively more accessible, with reporting and accessible emergency information? |
| Equality and employment law | Does the practice discriminate, and have required individual accommodations been provided? |
| Data protection law | Is personal data processing lawful, necessary, transparent and protected, with applicable decision-making safeguards? |

**These obligations can accumulate. A pass under one does not supply a pass under the others.** An accessible recruitment form can feed an exclusionary ranking model. An accurate model can be embedded in an inaccessible application. A correctly labelled synthetic video can still have unusable captions. These are separate findings and need separate remedies.

The strongest organisational response is a shared evidence and governance process with distinct legal conclusions. This is a proposed delivery approach, not a requirement to purchase a particular framework or establish one prescribed committee.

The article also understates an opportunity. AI can support alternative formats, communication, comprehension and task execution. But calling a tool “assistive” does not establish safety, accuracy, accessibility or an exemption. Benefits need evaluation alongside possible harms, with meaningful user choice.

## 2. Claim-by-claim review of the article

The legal references below link to the current consolidated framework or the Commission’s article-level explorer. See the [source register](#13-source-register) for the authentic amending regulation and supporting instruments.

| Article claim | Finding | More precise position |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Act is in force, with staged implementation through 2028. | Correct as a general introduction; incomplete for legacy systems. | The July 2026 amendment changes key dates. Some legacy-system provisions extend to 2030. |
| Consumer products containing AI in the EU are subject to the Act. | Too narrow as a scope description. | It also reaches professional, employment and public-service uses. Coverage follows the AI definition, operator role, territorial connection and exclusions. |
| High-risk providers must meet EAA and WAD accessibility requirements from December 2027. | The substantive link is correct; the date is not universal. | Article 16(l) creates the express duty. Relevant Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply from 2 December 2027 for Annex III systems and 2 August 2028 for Article 6(1)/Annex I systems, subject to scope and transitions. |
| In practice this means EN 301 549 and WCAG 2.2. | Useful direction, legally overcompressed. | Record the standard’s edition, covered requirements and legislation-specific Official Journal citation. WCAG alone does not cover the whole ICT or AI governance obligation. |
| A provider develops and markets AI. | Incomplete. | Developing or having a system developed and putting it into service under one’s own name can also qualify, including own use. |
| A deployer integrates third-party AI. | Too narrow. | Deployment concerns use under an organisation’s authority; technical integration is not necessary. A business may hold both roles for different systems. |
| There are four risk levels. | Acceptable teaching shorthand. | The legal obligations overlap. Transparency and general-purpose AI model rules do not fit neatly into four exclusive boxes. |
| AI literacy is a common organisational duty. | Correct, with an important 2026 qualification. | Amended Article 4 requires measures supporting literacy development; it does not require guaranteeing a particular individual proficiency level. |
| AI exploiting disability must not be used. | Correct only with the statutory conditions retained. | Article 5(1)(b) requires exploitation, material distortion of behaviour and significant harm or its reasonable likelihood. Ordinary helpful personalisation is not automatically prohibited. |
| Workplace emotion recognition is prohibited. | Broadly correct, but incomplete. | The prohibition also concerns education institutions and has medical or safety exceptions. Not every sentiment analysis tool meets the Act’s emotion-recognition definition. |
| New intimate-image and CSAM prohibitions apply from December 2026. | Supported by the enacted amendment. | The date is 2 December 2026. The detailed definitions, consent conditions and limited statutory qualifications still matter. |
| High-risk AI makes or supports significant decisions. | Useful illustration, not the classification test. | Apply Article 6 and Annexes I/III. Some listed preparatory or procedural uses may qualify for a documented derogation; Annex III profiling remains high-risk. |
| All generated content must be labelled as AI. | Too broad. | Provider machine-readable marking, deepfake disclosure, public-interest text disclosure and direct-interaction notices are different duties, with different exceptions. |
| AI transparency information must be accessible. | Correct. | Article 50(5) also requires clear, distinguishable information by first interaction or exposure. |
| Minimal-risk AI has no specific duties. | Misleading without qualification. | It may avoid high-risk requirements but still attract literacy, transparency or other laws. A use cannot be classified from its product label alone. |
| Financial services must explain decisions and undertake an impact assessment. | Requires use-case qualification. | Article 27 covers specified deployers and uses, including creditworthiness and life/health insurance risk assessment and pricing. Article 86 has its own conditions. |
| EAA work puts organisations in good shape for the AI Act. | Partly true. | It supports access, evidence and procurement. It does not supply AI risk classification, model/data assurance, human oversight, AI conformity assessment or a qualifying fundamental rights impact assessment. |

## 3. Current law and application dates

### 3.1 The 2026 amendment is enacted

Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 is the AI Act. Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026. The report uses the consolidated position dated 27 July 2026, checked against that amendment. Describing the December 2027 date merely as a proposal would now be inaccurate.

| Date | Relevant milestone | Delivery implication |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 August 2024 | Original AI Act entered into force. | Distinguish entry into force from application of individual duties. |
| 2 February 2025 | Chapters I and II began applying, including literacy and original prohibited practices. | Current obligations, not a future readiness exercise. Literacy wording has since been amended. |
| 2 August 2025 | GPAI model obligations and specified governance provisions began applying. | Check the separate transition for models already marketed before this date. |
| 27 July 2026 | AI Omnibus entered into force. | Update sources, dates, contracts and training references. |
| 2 August 2026 | General application date, including Article 50 transparency, subject to exceptions. | Assess current notices and content workflows. |
| 2 December 2026 | New Article 5 intimate-material/CSAM provisions apply; Article 111(4) transition ends for Article 50(2) marking by relevant pre-existing generators. | The marking transition is not a blanket postponement of all Article 50 obligations. |
| 2 August 2027 | Compliance deadline for GPAI models placed on the market before 2 August 2025. | Distinguish the model from systems built on it. |
| 2 December 2027 | Relevant Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply to Article 6(2)/Annex III high-risk systems. | Includes the provider accessibility duty and relevant deployer/FRIA obligations. |
| 2 August 2028 | Relevant Chapter III Sections 1–3 apply to Article 6(1)/Annex I high-risk systems. | Product qualification and sectoral integration require separate review. |
| 2030, for specified legacy cases | Article 111 contains later transitional provisions. | Record market/service dates, design changes and public-authority use. |

Article 111(2) addresses pre-existing high-risk systems and significant changes to their designs. It also sets an ultimate 2 August 2030 requirement for providers and deployers of high-risk systems intended for public authorities. Article 111(1) has a separate 31 December 2030 rule for specified large-scale IT components. Neither provision excuses prohibited practices or suspends independent accessibility/equality obligations.

Sources: [amending regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2026/1744/oj/eng); [Commission implementation timeline](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai); [Articles 111 and 113](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

### 3.2 Standards: three different questions

For every assessment ask: **Has the standard been published? Has its reference been cited for this legislation? Which requirements does that citation cover?**

| Source/version | Verified position at the cut-off | Proper use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| WCAG 2.2 | A published W3C Recommendation. | A strong web delivery baseline; not automatically the complete legal requirement under every EU instrument. |
| EN 301 549 v3.2.1 | Established harmonised reference under WAD. | Use the WAD relationship mapping and relevant clauses, including requirements beyond WCAG. Do not transfer WAD legal status to EAA. |
| EN 301 549 v4.1.1, September 2026 | Published; incorporates WCAG 2.2 and an EAA relationship annex. | Use for technical preparation and mapping. The reviewed sources did not establish a completed Official Journal citation at the cut-off. |
| ETSI work programme | Records delivery to the Commission on 30 October 2026 and a next-stage OJ citation target of 22 January 2027. | A planning/status record, not evidence that legal citation has already occurred or that the target will be met. |
| EN 17161 | Design-for-all process guidance relevant to organisational capability. | Useful supporting method; adoption does not certify a product, service or AI system. |

The Commission-supported AccessibleEU update correctly separates publication from legal reference, but its wording about v3.2.1 remaining “the current reference” should not be read as proving EAA harmonisation. The Commission’s WAD guidance and Double-FW’s dated standards register are more precise about that distinction.

Recommended policy: adopt WCAG 2.2 A/AA and applicable current EN requirements as delivery targets, while retaining the exact legal baseline and transition separately. If an older applicable baseline retains a requirement removed from a newer edition, preserve that difference rather than assuming automatic equivalence.

## 4. How the AI Act works

### 4.1 Start with the system, use and operator

An AI inventory should distinguish a foundation model, a configured application, the interface, connected tools and the service decision in which output is used. Buying an API does not make all downstream responsibilities disappear.

A provider can develop a system, commission its development, or put it into service under its own name. A deployer uses a system under its authority, apart from excluded personal non-professional use. Importers, distributors, authorised representatives and product manufacturers have additional roles.

Article 25 can move high-risk provider responsibilities downstream when a party rebrands, substantially modifies or changes the intended purpose of a system so it becomes high-risk. Ordinary configuration is not automatically a substantial modification; assess the actual change and legal definition. Contracts need to make necessary documentation, limitations, testing access and assistance available.

The territorial rules extend beyond EU-established suppliers, including specified third-country operators whose outputs are used in the EU. There are exclusions and tailored rules for areas including national security, certain research activities and qualifying open-source arrangements. Neither “open source” nor “research” should be used as an unexamined universal exemption.

Sources: [Article 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2); [Article 25](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-25).

### 4.2 Assess prohibitions before mitigation

Article 5 addresses specified manipulation, exploitation, social scoring, certain criminal-risk prediction, facial-image scraping, emotion inference, biometric categorisation and remote biometric identification practices. Its prohibitions have precise elements and exceptions. The new intimate-material provisions add further prohibited practices from December 2026.

Disability is expressly relevant to exploitation of vulnerabilities. For example, a sales assistant deliberately exploiting an identified cognitive vulnerability to induce harmful purchases warrants an Article 5 analysis. That is different from a user choosing a simpler explanation. Being disabled does not imply lack of capacity or susceptibility to manipulation.

For workplace or education emotion inference, assess the actual biometric functionality, intended purpose and any claimed medical/safety exception. Relabelling an inference as “engagement” or obtaining employee agreement does not, by itself, establish lawfulness. Conversely, a user’s self-reported satisfaction score is not automatically biometric emotion recognition.

Recommended control: prohibit the affected practice where the legal test is met; do not put it through a routine risk-acceptance process as though testing could legalise it.

Source: [Article 5](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-5).

### 4.3 High-risk classification has two principal routes

The first route combines a product/safety-component connection to Annex I legislation with the relevant third-party conformity-assessment condition. The second is the Annex III list, covering areas such as biometrics, infrastructure, education, employment, essential services, law enforcement, migration and justice/democratic processes.

Recruitment filtering and candidate evaluation, specified benefit decisions, creditworthiness and life/health insurance uses are particularly relevant to disability inclusion. The fact that a human signs the final decision does not automatically remove an AI-supported use from scope.

Article 6(3) allows certain Annex III uses to be treated as non-high-risk where its conditions are met, including lack of significant risk and qualifying procedural/preparatory characteristics. The provider must document its reasoning. Annex III profiling remains high-risk. A clerical support tool should not be equated with a ranking tool without examining what it actually does.

Source: [Article 6](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-6); [Annex III](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

### 4.4 Provider assurance is a lifecycle obligation

| Requirement area | Legal anchor | Disability-related question to investigate |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Risk management | Article 9 | What foreseeable harms affect disabled people, including in misuse and failure? |
| Data governance | Article 10 | Do data, labels, assumptions and gaps distort performance for relevant users? |
| Technical documentation and records | Articles 11–12 | Can the assessed version, conditions and results be reconstructed? |
| Instructions and transparency to deployers | Article 13 | Are limitations, supported uses and instructions accessible and understandable? |
| Human oversight | Article 14 | Can a competent person interpret, disregard, override or stop the system? |
| Accuracy, robustness and cybersecurity | Article 15 | Is performance resilient across relevant interaction differences and hostile conditions? |
| Accessibility | Article 16(l) | Does the high-risk system meet accessibility requirements in accordance with EAA/WAD? |
| Quality and corrective action | Articles 17–21 | Is there an operational method for finding and correcting recurring exclusion? |
| Conformity, declaration, marking and registration | Articles 43–49 | Has the correct route been followed for this classification? |
| Monitoring and serious incidents | Articles 72–73 | Are harmful changes detected and qualifying incidents routed appropriately? |

The questions in the third column are proposed assessment prompts, not quotations or independent legal tests.

Many Annex III systems use an internal-control conformity route. It would be wrong to tell every supplier to obtain an external AI accessibility certificate. Equally, internal assessment still requires substantive evidence. A CE mark, accessibility statement or supplier questionnaire is not a substitute for examining the particular system and use.

Sources: [Articles 9](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-9), [10](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-10), [13](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-13), [14](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14), [15](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-15), [16](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-16); [consolidated Articles 43–49 and 72–73](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

### 4.5 Deployers remain accountable for actual use

Article 26 requires appropriate use, competent and authorised human oversight, relevant input-data controls where the deployer controls inputs, operational monitoring and specified notifications. It also covers logs under the deployer’s control, generally for at least six months subject to applicable law, worker information and relevant public-authority registration.

For Annex III systems making or assisting decisions about individuals, the information duty is not satisfied merely by a general corporate AI policy. The affected person must receive the information required for the actual use. Accessible procurement evidence should therefore be supplemented by deployment testing and working support procedures.

Source: [Article 26](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26).

### 4.6 Fundamental rights impact assessment is selective

Article 27 does not require every high-risk deployer to perform a FRIA. It covers specified public-law bodies, private entities providing public services, and deployers of Annex III point 5(b)/(c) creditworthiness and life/health insurance systems, with an exclusion for the specified critical-infrastructure category.

The assessment addresses deployment processes, timing/frequency, affected groups, harms, oversight and responses including complaint arrangements. It must be updated when relevant facts change, with the required authority notification. The amended provision permits cross-references to relevant DPIA sections; it does not make data protection assessment automatically sufficient for all fundamental rights.

Recommended disability analysis should include who cannot enter the process, what proxies disadvantage people, whether accommodations work, whether an erroneous result can be challenged and whether harm remains after a technical fix.

Source: [Article 27](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-27).

### 4.7 Transparency is several duties, not one label

| Activity | Responsible actor and requirement | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Direct interaction with people | Providers design systems so people are informed they are interacting with AI. | An obviousness exception exists, assessed in context; other exceptions are specified. |
| Synthetic audio, images, video or text | Providers ensure outputs are machine-readably marked and detectable. | Technical feasibility and specified editing/other exceptions matter. |
| Permitted emotion-recognition or biometric-categorisation use | Deployers inform exposed people. | This does not authorise a prohibited use. |
| Deepfake image, audio or video | Deployers disclose artificial generation/manipulation. | Creative/fictional work has a tailored disclosure approach, not blanket immunity. |
| AI-generated/manipulated text informing the public on public-interest matters | Deployers disclose. | Human review/editorial control plus editorial responsibility can engage an exception; it is not universal compulsory labelling of all AI-assisted writing. |

Article 50(5) requires clear, distinguishable and accessible information at the latest by first interaction/exposure. A machine-readable marker and an accessible human notice serve different purposes; one should not be assumed to supply the other.

Recommended implementation: maintain a content/use register showing the applicable paragraph, actor, exception rationale, human notice, machine marking where relevant and evidence that transformations preserve them.

Source: [Article 50](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-50).

### 4.8 General-purpose AI needs a separate workstream

The article omits the model-level regime. Article 53 addresses documentation, information for downstream providers, copyright policy and training-content summaries, with qualified open-source exceptions. Article 55 adds evaluation, systemic-risk mitigation, incident and cybersecurity duties for models with systemic risk.

A chatbot’s use of a compliant model does not prove the chatbot is accessible. A model-level document may nevertheless reveal limitations relevant to dysarthric speech, minority languages, image description or erroneous advice. Downstream teams should request useful evidence rather than treating model branding as assurance.

Sources: [Article 53](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-53); [Article 55](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-55).

### 4.9 Enforcement and remedies matter to accessibility

The framework includes national competent authorities, market surveillance and Commission/AI Office functions; the 2026 amendment expands certain AI Office powers. Responsibilities depend on the operator and system. Article 85 provides a market-surveillance complaint route. Article 86 provides a conditional right to clear, meaningful explanations for specified adversely affecting Annex III decisions, excluding point 2 and subject to its legal qualifications.

Article 99 includes ceilings of €35 million/7% for prohibited practices, €15 million/3% for listed operator obligations, and €7.5 million/1% for misleading/incomplete information. These are ceilings with calculation, proportionality and SME/SMC qualifications, not automatic penalties. The GPAI regime has separate enforcement provisions. Avoid using the largest headline figure for every accessibility defect.

Sources: [Article 86](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-86); [Article 99](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-99); [consolidated enforcement provisions](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

### 4.10 Voluntary practice and innovation support

Article 95 expressly supports voluntary codes addressing inclusive and diverse design, stakeholder participation and adverse impacts on vulnerable groups, including disability accessibility. This provides a useful route for extending good governance to systems outside the high-risk regime. A voluntary code does not displace mandatory transparency, equality or accessibility duties.

The Act also provides sandboxes, real-world testing arrangements and SME support. These mechanisms facilitate supervised innovation rather than declare tested systems compliant. Real-world high-risk testing has specific safeguards, including appropriate protection for vulnerable participants. Ordinary usability research and regulated real-world AI testing are not automatically the same activity; classify the proposed study before choosing its process.

Sources: [Article 95](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-95); [consolidated Articles 57–63](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

## 5. Accessibility and disability throughout the AI lifecycle

### 5.1 Four things need different evidence

| Assurance question | Example failure | Suitable evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Can the person use the interface? | Streaming messages repeatedly interrupt screen-reader speech. | Technical assessment, manual AT testing and interaction observations. |
| Is the AI output usable and accurate for the purpose? | A simplified benefits explanation removes an eligibility qualification. | Source comparison, expert review and comprehension testing. |
| Is the decision or service process equitable? | Disability-related employment gaps lower applicant rankings. | Validity/proxy analysis, relevant subgroup evaluation and accommodation review. |
| Can people retain control and seek remedy? | A rejected applicant can only appeal through an inaccessible chatbot. | Complete challenge journeys, oversight exercises and accessible communications. |

This four-part model is an analytical synthesis. It prevents a narrow WCAG report from being used to answer questions it did not investigate.

Article 16(l) is the direct accessibility hook. Recital 80 explains the relationship with the UN disability convention, dignity, privacy, universal design and accessibility by design. A recital supports interpretation; it is not a separate universal technical checklist.

Sources: [Article 16](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-16); [Recital 80](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/recital-80).

### 5.2 Disability fairness cannot be reduced to one dataset field

Relevant differences can include speech, sight, hearing, mobility, cognition, fluctuating conditions, assistive technology, language and intersections. One aggregate “disabled/non-disabled” comparison can conceal serious differences. Conversely, small groups do not justify statistically confident claims from inadequate data.

Potential harms to investigate include speech differences treated as incompetence; eye contact interpreted as engagement; interaction speed used as a proxy for ability; inaccessible assessments generating misleading performance data; and inaccessible earlier journeys making disabled people underrepresented in training data.

These are risk hypotheses, not findings about a particular product. Each needs evidence relevant to the intended purpose. There is no general legal requirement for equal numerical outcomes across every group, nor a universal disability sampling quota in the AI Act.

Source for data-governance duties: [Article 10](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-10).

### 5.3 The new sensitive-data provision is important

Amended Article 4a permits exceptional processing of special-category personal data for strictly necessary bias detection and correction, subject to cumulative safeguards. It extends the route beyond high-risk providers under specified conditions. It does not create a general duty for other systems to undertake this processing.

Safeguards include demonstrating that other data cannot effectively meet the purpose, technical restrictions on reuse, security, controlled access, no third-party transmission/transfer/access under this route, deletion conditions and a documented necessity rationale. Relevant GDPR requirements still apply.

An accessibility preference or assistive-technology signal may reveal health/disability information in context. Do not assume preference data is always innocuous, that pseudonymisation makes it anonymous, or that a customer survey supplies permission to share it with an external AI model. Separate research consent, operational personalisation and model improvement.

Sources: [Article 4a](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-4a); [GDPR](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng).

### 5.4 Oversight must itself be accessible

A nominal human reviewer who cannot use the dashboard, understand confidence information or intervene in time cannot provide effective oversight. Recommended delivery therefore covers operator interfaces as well as customer interfaces: readable explanations, meaningful alerts, keyboard/AT access and practicable stop/override procedures.

Human involvement is not automatically meaningful. Exercise a case where the AI is wrong and check that the reviewer recognises the problem, has authority to change the outcome and records what happened. The exact required controls depend on Article 14’s proportionality and system context.

Sources: [Article 14](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-14); [Article 26](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-26).

## 6. Parallels with accessibility and disability legislation

### 6.1 EAA: service outcomes, interoperability and continuing conformity

The EAA is Directive (EU) 2019/882, implemented through national law. Its coverage is a defined set of products and consumer services; it is not a general law for every website or all workplace software. Most implementing measures apply from 28 June 2025, with particular transitions and exceptions.

For AI-enabled covered services, relevant Annex I outcomes include accessible information, perceivable/operable/understandable/robust digital interfaces, accessible support and sector-specific functions. Banking includes accessible identification/security/payment and a B2 complexity ceiling for information. Media-access services include accessible programme guides and preservation of access features.

Section VII’s functional criteria cover use without vision, with limited vision, without colour perception, without/with limited hearing, without speech, with limited manipulation/reach/strength, with seizure risk and with limited cognition, plus privacy when using accessibility features. They are functional coverage criteria, **not a diagnostic customer taxonomy or permission to infer disability**. Their use as alternatives to technical requirements is constrained by the Annex.

The practical parallel is continual conformity: service design, information, monitoring, change and corrective action. An AI model or renderer update should trigger assessment of affected functions. EAA exemptions do not automatically exempt an operator from the AI Act or equality law.

Sources: [EAA Articles 2, 4, 13–15 and Annex I](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng); [functional criteria](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2019/882/oj/eng#anx_I).

### 6.2 WAD: public interfaces and accountable feedback

WAD addresses covered public-sector websites/apps, including accessibility statements, feedback routes, monitoring and enforcement. It provides a useful operating model for honest disclosure of known barriers and requests for accessible information.

A benefits chatbot can engage WAD at the interface and AI Act high-risk requirements when it evaluates eligibility. A chatbot that merely explains published opening hours is a different AI use. WAD includes exclusions, including public-service broadcasters and their subsidiaries; a media organisation is not automatically covered merely because it is publicly funded. Other media, accessibility and national obligations may still apply.

Source: [WAD, particularly Articles 1 and 4–9](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2016/2102/oj/eng).

### 6.3 AVMSD: content access and delivered quality

“AVMS” is treated here as the Audiovisual Media Services Directive, Directive 2010/13/EU as amended, particularly by Directive (EU) 2018/1808.

Article 7 requires Member States to ensure continuous, progressive accessibility through proportionate measures, regular provider reporting, accessible complaint contact arrangements and accessible emergency information. States encourage accessibility action plans. Detailed quotas, quality rules and licence conditions require jurisdiction-specific verification; there is no single universal EU caption percentage to import into an AI policy.

The division of responsibility is crucial: **AVMSD concerns accessibility of the media service; EAA separately addresses covered services providing access to audiovisual media; the AI Act can address the AI tools and synthetic content involved.**

For AI-assisted captions, translation, signing or description, investigate meaning, errors, timing, language and audience comprehension. The existence of an output file does not establish access. For synthetic presenters or manipulated footage, assess Article 50’s deepfake definition, disclosure duties and creative-work qualifications separately. Do not assume every synthesised access-service voice is necessarily a deepfake.

Source: [current AVMSD, Article 7](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:02010L0013-20181218).

Recommended media acceptance should trace the approved source, generated access asset, reviewed master, packaged/transcoded version, distribution route, player and actual audience experience. A correct caption file that never reaches the viewer fails delivery. A compliant AI disclosure does not cure that failure.

### 6.4 Employment equality and reasonable accommodation

Directive 2000/78/EC prohibits relevant employment discrimination, including disability discrimination, and Article 5 provides for reasonable accommodation. National implementing rules determine detailed application.

This operates alongside the AI Act. Avoiding a prohibited emotion-inference tool is only one part of the analysis. A hiring process may still disadvantage a candidate because an assessment measures inaccessible interaction demands rather than the job’s requirements. A generally accessible platform can still need an individual accommodation.

Recommended controls include job-related assessment validity, an accessible adjustment route, equivalent alternatives, protection against penalising assistive-technology use and a meaningful review of adverse decisions. Not every private employer deploying recruitment AI is automatically subject to Article 27’s FRIA duty; equality obligations and other assessments can still apply.

Source: [Directive 2000/78/EC, Articles 1–5](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2000/78/oj/eng).

### 6.5 GDPR: privacy, inference and significant decisions

GDPR adds lawful-basis, special-category, transparency, minimisation, security and DPIA questions. Article 22 addresses decisions based solely on automated processing with legal or similarly significant effects, subject to exceptions and safeguards. It is not identical to the AI Act’s high-risk classification.

A human signature does not answer whether the involvement is substantive; inspect the process. Likewise, satisfying an Article 22 exception does not establish AI Act or equality compliance. Prefer asking people what interaction support they want over inferring diagnoses from their behaviour. Preserve accessible privacy information and rights-request routes.

Source: [GDPR, particularly Articles 5–6, 9, 12–15, 22, 25 and 35](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2016/679/oj/eng).

### 6.6 UNCRPD and the EU Charter

The Convention on the Rights of Persons with Disabilities provides the wider rights foundation: accessibility, access to information, work, participation and privacy. It places obligations on States Parties; the route to an enforceable private-sector obligation must be identified through applicable law. It is not a substitute technical certification scheme.

The Charter’s Article 21 addresses non-discrimination and Article 26 disability independence/integration/participation. Its application must respect Article 51’s scope. Together with AI Act Recital 80, these sources support assessing people affected by AI decisions, not only those operating an interface.

Sources: [UNCRPD Article 9](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-9-%20accessibility.html); [Article 21](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-21-freedom-of-expression-and-opinion-and-access-to-information.html); [Article 27](https://www.un.org/development/desa/disabilities/convention-on-the-rights-of-persons-with-disabilities/article-27-work-and-employment.html); [Charter](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/PDF/?from=EN&uri=CELEX%3A12012P%2FTXT); [Recital 80](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/recital-80).

### 6.7 Public procurement

Article 42 of Directive 2014/24/EU requires relevant technical specifications for use by natural persons to consider disability accessibility/design for all, except in duly justified cases, and refers to mandatory Union accessibility requirements where adopted. This reaches tools for staff as well as the public.

Recommended AI procurement therefore asks for tested versions, actual integration evidence, instructions, known limitations, model/data information appropriate to the buyer’s role, change notices, incident cooperation and an exit route. A VPAT/accessibility conformance report can organise evidence; it is not an AI Act declaration or a universal EU statutory reporting format.

Source: [Public Procurement Directive, Article 42](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/ALL/?uri=CELEX:02014L0024-20240101).

### 6.8 Communications, emergency access and assistive products

The European Electronic Communications Code, particularly Articles 109 and 111, adds emergency and equivalent-access/choice requirements. EAA adds relevant real-time text, total-conversation and emergency-answering requirements. An AI voice service should not be assumed to replace a required accessible communications mode. Emergency triage AI can also engage Annex III classification.

An assistive AI product with a medical intended purpose may engage medical-device law. The AI Act product route depends on the actual qualification and conformity-assessment conditions. “Used by a disabled person” does not itself make an app a medical device or high-risk AI.

Sources: [EECC](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2018/1972/oj/eng); [Medical Devices Regulation](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2017/745/oj/eng); [AI Act Article 6](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-6).

### 6.9 UK implications

UK-established organisations can fall within the EU AI Act’s territorial rules. This does not make EU requirements a replacement for domestic duties. Great Britain’s Equality Act and public-sector accessibility requirements require their own assessment; Northern Ireland has a distinct legal framework.

For Double-FW’s work, keep the EU market/use profile and UK employment/service profile as separate applicability records. Current UK government guidance confirms reasonable-adjustment duties in employment and protection across recruitment and employment processes.

Sources: [AI Act Article 2](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-2); [UK disability employment guidance](https://www.gov.uk/rights-disabled-person/employment); [reasonable adjustments](https://www.gov.uk/reasonable-adjustments-for-disabled-workers).

### 6.10 Consumer protection and manipulation

The Unfair Commercial Practices Directive already addresses foreseeable distortion of behaviour among specified vulnerable consumer groups. This parallels the AI Act's concern with exploitation, but the scope and tests differ. A sales practice that does not meet Article 5's significant-harm conditions can still require consumer-law analysis.

Recommended review should examine misleading authority claims, fabricated urgency, pressure to purchase, inaccessible cancellation and exploiting a user's request for support. Do not assume that disability itself establishes vulnerability in every transaction. Assess the person, practice and context without stereotyping.

Source: [Directive 2005/29/EC, particularly Article 5(3)](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2005/29/oj/eng).

### 6.11 Platforms and recommender systems

The Digital Services Act adds duties for relevant intermediary/platform services. Article 26(3) prohibits covered platforms from presenting advertisements based on profiling using GDPR special-category data. Article 27 addresses recommender transparency; very large services have further risk/mitigation and recommender-choice duties. Applicability and size-related exceptions need their own assessment.

This qualifies the article's description of recommendations as minimal risk. They may avoid AI Act high-risk classification while still attracting substantial platform and privacy obligations. Do not convert accessibility preferences into disability-based advertising profiles under the guise of personalisation.

Source: [DSA, Articles 26–27 and relevant very-large-service provisions](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2022/2065/oj/eng).

### 6.12 Accessible-format copyright exceptions

Directive (EU) 2017/1564 and Regulation (EU) 2017/1563 implement the Marrakesh framework for specified beneficiaries, works, accessible-format uses and exchanges. They are relevant where AI helps produce reading formats for blind, visually impaired or otherwise print-disabled people.

The framework is not a general permission to upload an entire collection to a commercial AI service or train a model. Check lawful access, beneficiary/authorised-entity status, permitted use, distribution and data processing. The AI Act's GPAI copyright obligations and accessibility's public-interest purpose should be considered together without assuming either removes the other's conditions.

Sources: [Directive 2017/1564](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32017L1564); [Regulation 2017/1563](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX:32017R1563); [AI Act Article 53](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-53).

## 7. Evaluation of every directly linked resource

These are content/reliability evaluations, not accessibility audits of the websites. Specialist commercial pages demonstrate what is offered, not independent proof of its quality or legal sufficiency.

### 7.1 WAD legal text

[Directive (EU) 2016/2102](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/dir/2016/2102/oj/eng) is an appropriate primary source. Its value is the exact scope, exclusions, obligations and monitoring structure. It should be read with national implementing measures and current standards citations. A link to it does not establish that every public-sector entity or every AI system falls within its independent scope.

**Disposition: retain as a primary legal source.** Add an article-level pointer and applicable national measures in live assessments.

### 7.2 “Understanding the EAA”

[The March 2025 article](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2025/03/19/understanding-the-eaa/) provides a useful tour of requirements and continuing service responsibilities. However, the reviewed version still describes the standards updates as unpublished, which is outdated for EN 301 549 v4.1.1 at this report’s cut-off.

It also overcompresses the EAA/AVMSD division by describing TV/video services broadly. Its Annex I Section VI explanation is misleading: that section concerns the presumption that requirements in other Union acts are met in specified circumstances, not general conformity with other EU standards. The suggestion that the EAA does not itself state general accessibility requirements obscures Annex I.

**Disposition: useful orientation with corrections; do not adopt as the controlling scope or standards register.**

### 7.3 “WCAG primer”

[The primer](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2020/04/10/wcag-primer/) explains principles, conformance and web accessibility in approachable language. Its value is staff understanding. It is not a complete EN assessment, an AI fairness method or an AI conformity route.

**Disposition: retain for education; pair with actual criteria, applicable EN clauses and AI-specific assessment.**

### 7.4 Article 4 on artificialintelligenceact.eu

[This Article 4 page](https://artificialintelligenceact.eu/article/4/) is an independent explanatory/text service, not an EU institution. The reviewed site includes amended-Act navigation and is useful for discovery. Current obligations should be checked against EUR-Lex and the Commission’s explorer. The important current wording is support for literacy development, with no requirement to guarantee individual attainment.

**Disposition: useful secondary navigation; use the official text for decisions.**

### 7.5 “Foundations: types of disability”

[The foundations article](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2024/11/18/foundations-types-of-disability/) helps teams think beyond a single interaction mode. Its seeing/hearing/moving/thinking categories are introductory, not a complete legal, cultural or research classification.

Some examples risk overgeneralisation: Deaf people’s communication preferences differ, captions do not replace sign-language access for everyone, and speech recognition may be unsuitable for someone whose condition affects speech. Situational barriers can illuminate design needs without being equated with every legal definition of disability. Include fatigue, pain, fluctuating needs and intersections.

**Disposition: awareness material; avoid using its examples to predict an individual’s needs.**

### 7.6 Board accessibility governance

[The August 2026 governance article](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2026/08/10/board-getting-accessibility-governance-right/) makes a strong operational case for ownership, risk registers, investment, procurement and complaints analysis. Its emphasis on evidence supports AI governance.

The extension needed is model/data/decision oversight and an explicit distinction between board risk management and permission to depart from law. OECD principles and national governance codes have different scope and legal effects; they do not impose one identical duty on every organisation. Commercial exposure should complement disability rights, rather than determine whether exclusion matters.

**Disposition: strong governance framing; supplement with role-specific statutory duties and funded controls.**

### 7.7 Assessments service page

[The assessments page](https://tetralogical.com/services/assessments/) describes sampling, standards assessment and AT testing. These are relevant methods. AI review additionally needs dynamic output, repeated trials, decision/data risks and operational change assessment. Sampling limits should be explicit.

**Disposition: relevant commercial offering; it is not independent evidence that a given assessment satisfies the whole AI Act.**

### 7.8 Agile usability testing service page

[The usability-testing page](https://tetralogical.com/services/agile-usability-testing/) describes disabled participant research aligned to delivery cycles. That supports discovery, comprehension and interaction evaluation. It does not replace criterion-level assessment, specialist model evaluation or a FRIA.

**Disposition: relevant research method; require accessible participation, appropriate coverage, ethical data handling and reported limitations.**

### 7.9 EAA FAQ

[The July 2025 FAQ](https://tetralogical.com/blog/2025/07/18/eaa-faq/) is useful for questions about service responsibilities and national enforcement. Several statements need correction:

- Its decision tree asks whether the organisation has at least ten employees **and** turnover of at least €2 million. That is not the inverse of the EAA microenterprise definition.
- The definition is fewer than ten persons **and** turnover **or** balance-sheet total not exceeding €2 million. The service-provider exemption and the treatment of product operators must be distinguished. Group/enterprise facts may require additional analysis.
- Saying a directive is “not a law” is misleading. It is EU legislation binding Member States as to the result; national implementation determines many operator-facing details.
- Standards, harmonisation and publication status need updating and legislation-specific verification.
- Annex V service information is not automatically identical to WAD’s prescribed model accessibility statement. National rules may require more.
- Disproportionate-burden assessment is an operator assessment under Article 14 with documentation, review and notification conditions; it should not be described simply as applying for permission to be exempt.

**Disposition: do not use the decision tree as an exclusion filter; correct before reusing it.**

### 7.10 AI and WCAG tag pages

[The AI tag index](https://tetralogical.com/blog/tag/artificial-intelligence/) and [WCAG tag index](https://tetralogical.com/blog/tag/wcag/) are discovery aids. They link to changing collections and do not independently substantiate legal claims. The AI index’s related writing usefully highlights inaccurate generated code/descriptions and the need for accessibility expertise.

**Disposition: use to discover reading; cite the specific dated item and primary source needed for a claim.**

## 9. An integrated delivery and evidence model

### 9.1 Minimum records

| Record | Minimum content | Why it matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| System/use inventory | Purpose, affected people, model/application/interface, tools, supplier, owner, jurisdictions and versions. | Prevents a model purchase from obscuring the service use. |
| Applicability decision | Actor, AI definition, territorial link, exclusions, classification, dates, exact source and reviewer. | Establishes which duties are being assessed. |
| Requirement register | Legal or adopted-policy source, user outcome, applicability, owner and evidence method. | Separates law from organisational ambition. |
| Risk/impact assessment | Harms, affected groups, data assumptions, controls, residual issues and FRIA/DPIA links. | Connects access and decision consequences. |
| Supplier agreement/evidence | Instructions, limitations, testing access, cooperation, changes and incident arrangements. | Supports downstream responsibilities. |
| Assessment results | Build/model/configuration, inputs/outputs, method, environment, observation and limitations. | Allows repeatable review and change comparison. |
| Decision/corrective record | Named authority, unresolved issues, action, verified retest and review trigger. | Keeps an open problem from becoming a documentary pass. |

Keep confidential operational evidence outside public knowledge repositories. Link records through protected identifiers. A generated approval field is not an authenticated decision.

### 9.2 A practical assurance sequence

1. Identify the use and affected people, including excluded non-users.
2. Resolve prohibitions, roles, scope and applicable dates.
3. Map AI, accessibility, equality, data and sector duties separately.
4. Specify the full interaction, decision, oversight and recovery journey.
5. Assess data/model risks and instructions alongside interface design.
6. Test deterministic behaviour with controlled fixtures; evaluate representative real-model outputs repeatedly.
7. Undertake relevant disabled-user research and specialist reviews.
8. Review evidence, resolve material barriers, complete the applicable formal processes and authorise release.
9. Monitor, investigate complaints, correct, retest and reopen decisions after relevant change.

This is a recommended sequence, not the Act’s prescribed project-management template. Different workstreams may run together where dependencies permit.

### 9.3 Tests and outcome measures

Use technical conformance, usability, output quality, fairness and governance measures together. Do not collapse them into one reassuring score.

| Dimension | Candidate measure | Interpretation limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Navigation | Discovery, orientation, history/source access and recovery. | A successful path does not prove all routes accessible. |
| Task completion | Independent completion, correctness and avoidable assistance. | Completion with excessive burden can still indicate exclusion. |
| Task effort/time | Steps, fatigue, interruption recovery and user-acceptable pace. | Speed is not a proxy for competence or disability. |
| Understanding | Correct interpretation of limits, options, consequences and decisions. | Satisfaction alone cannot establish understanding. |
| Preference support | User-chosen mode, persistence, easy reversal and privacy. | Do not infer diagnosis from chosen settings. |
| AI performance | Relevant error types, variation and subgroup/context performance. | State sample size, uncertainty and untested groups. |
| Agency/remedy | Stop, correct, override, appeal and human support outcomes. | A visible button does not establish effective intervention. |
| Media quality | Meaningful caption/AD/signing errors and delivered access. | Aggregate accuracy can hide a critical mistake. |

For AI-generated content, retain enough context to reproduce the evaluated condition: model/configuration, prompt, retrieved source revisions, renderer and relevant output. A temperature or model-version setting is not a reproducibility guarantee. Report observed variability and retest consequential changes.

### 9.4 Agentic delivery and robustness

The AI Act’s robustness concerns resilience, accuracy and cybersecurity. WCAG robustness concerns reliable interpretation, including by assistive technologies. They reinforce one another but are not the same requirement.

Recommended agent-enabled access adds stable semantics, accessible APIs where appropriate, permission boundaries, trustworthy sources, user control and recoverable actions. The Act does not generally require a website to expose an MCP server or unrestricted agent access.

For an agent acting for a disabled person, define the permitted action and data, confirmation for consequential steps where appropriate, a usable record, stop conditions and recovery. Test prompt injection, misleading source material and accidental disclosure. Accessibility customisation should not require broad account access or a medical profile.

Source for AI robustness: [Article 15](https://ai-act-service-desk.ec.europa.eu/en/ai-act/article-15). Supporting delivery guidance: [human/AI responsibilities](https://github.com/Double-FW/Accessibility_Programme_Runbooks/blob/main/framework/human-ai-responsibilities.md).

### 9.5 Monitoring and incident handling

Separate an ordinary accessibility defect, a consumer complaint, an emerging discriminatory pattern, a security/data incident and an AI Act serious incident. One event can enter several processes. The reporting threshold and clock must come from the applicable provision, not a generic policy.

For example, provider reporting under Article 73 generally has a 15-day outer limit, with shorter conditions for specified events; deployer duties under Article 26 also require prompt action and notifications. A known barrier should not wait for a monthly dashboard if people are being excluded now.

Recommended monitoring triggers include model changes, prompt/retrieval changes, SDK/renderer upgrades, supplier policy changes, new populations/languages, new actions, complaints and evidence of unequal performance. Preserve earlier negative results when an issue is fixed.

Sources: [consolidated Articles 26, 72–73](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/eli/reg/2024/1689/2026-07-27/eng).

## 10. Sector and use-case analysis

| Use case | AI Act analysis | Parallel duties and recommended evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Retail support chatbot | Often direct-interaction transparency; not high-risk merely because it is a chatbot. Added eligibility or credit functions change the analysis. | Covered e-commerce journey, accessible help, reliable product information, disclosures and transaction recovery. |
| Consumer credit assessment | Annex III creditworthiness/credit-score use, subject to its fraud-detection exception and classification rules. Relevant FRIA duties. | Accessible application/security, fair relevant inputs, meaningful explanations and challenge process. |
| Life/health insurance pricing | Listed risk assessment/pricing use; not all insurance is automatically included. | Accessible journey, disability/equality analysis, FRIA and appropriate evidence for underwriting decisions. |
| Candidate screening/ranking | Employment Annex III analysis; human final approval does not automatically remove classification. | Accommodation, job-related validity, accessible alternatives, worker/applicant information and review. |
| Benefits eligibility | Listed public-assistance evaluation uses can be high-risk; distinguish advice from eligibility evaluation. | WAD interface, public-service FRIA where applicable, comprehensible decision and usable appeal. |
| AI captions or description | Classify the tool’s use; apply synthetic-content rules where relevant rather than assuming all output requires the same label. | AVMSD/media rules, EAA access-service functions where covered, editorial review and transmission/player evidence. |
| AI sign-language avatar | Not automatically high-risk; assess actual use and applicable transparency conditions. | Competent language review, Deaf-user research, meaning, timing, readability and alternatives. An avatar’s presence is not proof of access. |
| Employee summarisation aid | May be lower-risk assistance; reassess if repurposed for performance scoring. | Optional accommodation, confidentiality, output verification, accessible non-AI alternative and trial outcomes. |
| Disability personalisation agent | No automatic exemption or high-risk classification solely because it assists disabled users. | Minimum data, user control, accessible permission/confirmation, safe actions and rollback. |
| Assistive medical product | Assess medical qualification and Article 6(1) conditions, including sector-specific rules. | Clinical/safety and usability evidence, accessible instructions and risk management. |

These are routing examples, not final classifications of unnamed products. The application’s purpose, deployment, effect and relevant exceptions determine the conclusion.

### Worked example: accessible recruitment

A supplier demonstrates a keyboard-accessible application and supplies an accessibility report. The employer then uses a video assessment to rank candidates.

The technical report may support some interface findings. It does not answer whether the ranking uses prohibited emotion inference, whether speech/appearance measures are valid for the job, whether disabled candidates can obtain accommodations or whether the reviewer can challenge a generated score. Ask for those separate facts before treating the supplier as approved for the use.

### Worked example: AI-assisted news video

A producer uses generative editing and automated captions. The broadcaster needs separate decisions about synthetic-content disclosure, editorial responsibility, caption accuracy, available access features and final delivery. A disclosure in the visual corner of a video may not be available to a blind viewer; a caption file with the correct format may still misstate a person’s name or reverse a statement. Evaluate both the notice and access content.

### Worked example: customer evidence driving an agent

A customer selects text interaction and step-by-step guidance. The service records satisfaction with navigation and completion. An improvement agent receives authorised aggregate findings plus design and governance guidance.

This can support improvement without giving the agent customer medical histories or individual authority to change live services. Evaluate whether the segmentation and small cells can reveal identity. Keep proposed changes reviewable and test their impact. This is a proposed architecture, not something EAA Annex I or the AI Act expressly prescribes.

## 12. Interpretation boundaries and outstanding checks

This report provides a researched framework, not a determination for a particular organisation, Member State or deployed system. Live decisions require facts about purpose, roles, affected people, countries, sector, versions and dates.

The main boundaries are:

- **Article 16(l):** its express high-risk accessibility duty should not be dismissed simply because a product falls outside the EAA’s independent consumer scope. Equally, the report does not claim that every EAA/WAD administrative procedure transfers wholesale to every AI provider. Map the accessibility duty, system functions and applicable implementation requirements precisely.
- **Standards status:** publication is verified; completed v4.1.1 OJ citation was not established at the cut-off. A planned date is not evidence of legal effect. Recheck the actual decision before a claim.
- **National implementation:** detailed EAA/WAD/AVMSD enforcement, exemptions, reporting and licence conditions need jurisdiction-specific sources.
- **Technical assurance:** no product audit, model evaluation, user study or supplier service delivery was executed for this report.
- **Repository maturity:** reviewed guidance ranges from established documents to draft frameworks. Templates and synthetic validation do not establish real-world effectiveness.
- **Coverage:** this is a substantive accessibility/disability analysis across the Act’s main regimes, not an exhaustive legal commentary on every procedural article, delegated measure or Member State implementation.
- **Future changes:** later amendments, standards citations and implementing guidance can alter the position. The report’s date is part of its meaning.

The central delivery judgement is that accessibility, fair decision-making, privacy, human control and remedy need connected evidence. No single audit score or framework can answer all five.
